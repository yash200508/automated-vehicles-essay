Are Automated Vehicles Truly Ready for Public Roads?

A clear argument for pausing self-driving cars until proven 100% safe.

The Problem

What Are Automated Vehicles?

Automated vehicles (AVs), also known as self-driving cars, are vehicles equipped with artificial intelligence, sensors, cameras, and radar systems designed to navigate roads without human intervention. They promise to reduce accidents caused by human error and revolutionize transportation.

Why Are They Controversial?

Despite industry claims, automated vehicles remain in an experimental phase. Current systems struggle with unpredictable scenarios, adverse weather conditions, and complex urban environments. Safety testing is incomplete, yet these vehicles are being deployed on public roads alongside pedestrians, cyclists, and human-driven vehicles.

The gap between marketing promises and engineering reality has created a dangerous situation where consumers and the public bear the risks of unfinished technology.

Companies exaggerate readiness; defects still kill people.

Evidence

Automated vehicles have been involved in hundreds of crashes, many resulting in serious injuries and fatalities. Federal agencies have documented a disturbing pattern of failures across multiple manufacturers.

Detailed Incident Reports

🚗

Tesla Autopilot Crashes

Multiple documented cases of Tesla vehicles on Autopilot crashing into barriers, stationary emergency vehicles, and parked cars. Several fatal crashes occurred when drivers over-relied on the system. In one case, a Tesla on Autopilot failed to detect a tractor-trailer crossing the highway, resulting in a fatality. Federal investigations revealed the system frequently fails to recognize stopped vehicles and objects in the roadway.

🚕

Waymo & Cruise Incidents in San Francisco

Robotaxis blocked intersections, prevented emergency vehicle access, and exhibited erratic behavior including sudden stops in traffic lanes. In October 2023, a Cruise vehicle struck a pedestrian who had been hit by another car, then dragged the victim 20 feet while attempting to pull over. This incident led to the suspension of Cruise's license to operate in California and sparked intense regulatory scrutiny.

⚠️

Uber Fatal Crash (2018) - Tempe, Arizona

The first pedestrian fatality caused by a self-driving car. An Uber autonomous vehicle struck and killed 49-year-old Elaine Herzberg as she crossed the street with her bicycle. Investigation revealed that while the vehicle's sensors detected her presence 6 seconds before impact, the system failed to correctly classify her as a pedestrian and did not engage emergency braking. The backup safety driver was watching a video on their phone at the time of the crash.

🚨

Tesla Crashes into Emergency Vehicles

At least 12 documented cases of Tesla vehicles on Autopilot crashing into parked police cars, fire trucks, and ambulances with flashing lights. Despite the bright emergency lighting, the Autopilot system failed to recognize these stationary vehicles as hazards, leading to high-speed collisions. These incidents prompted NHTSA to open a formal investigation into Autopilot's detection capabilities.

"Public streets have become laboratories for unfinished technology."

Ethics and the Responsibility Gap

One of the most troubling aspects of automated vehicle deployment is the responsibility gap — a moral and legal vacuum where accountability becomes unclear when accidents occur.

Traditional vehicles place responsibility squarely on human drivers. With AVs, however, blame becomes diffused among manufacturers, software developers, vehicle owners, and the AI systems themselves. This creates a situation where victims of AV crashes face significant challenges in seeking justice and compensation.

⚖️
"The responsibility gap — where moral accountability disappears between human and machine."

Furthermore, AVs are programmed with decision-making algorithms that may prioritize certain outcomes over others in unavoidable crash scenarios. Who decides these ethical frameworks? Should a vehicle prioritize the safety of its occupants over pedestrians? These questions remain largely unanswered, yet the technology continues to be deployed.

Counterpoints

The Claim
Automated Vehicles Will Reduce Human Error
Proponents argue that since human error causes approximately 94% of traffic accidents, removing human drivers will dramatically improve road safety. They cite the potential for AVs to eliminate drunk driving, distracted driving, and other forms of impaired operation.
The Rebuttal
Software Errors Are Systematic and Unpredictable
While human errors are individual and localized, software defects can affect thousands of vehicles simultaneously. A single coding error or algorithmic flaw can replicate across entire fleets, creating systematic risks that are harder to predict and mitigate than individual human mistakes.

Additionally, the assumption that AVs will eventually be "safer" than humans fails to address the current reality: they are not yet ready. The question is not whether AVs might someday be beneficial, but whether it is ethical to use the public as unwitting test subjects during their development phase.

We Must Pause Deployment Until AVs Are Truly Safe

The evidence is clear: automated vehicles are not ready for widespread public deployment. While the promise of safer roads is compelling, the current reality is that these systems remain experimental, with real human costs.

We need comprehensive safety standards, transparent testing protocols, clear accountability frameworks, and independent verification before allowing AVs to operate freely on public roads.

Until automated vehicles can demonstrate 100% safety — or at minimum, safety levels significantly exceeding human drivers under all conditions — a moratorium on public deployment is not just prudent; it is a moral imperative.
